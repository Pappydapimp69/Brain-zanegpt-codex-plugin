## E1 — raw vs jpeg tonal headroom synthetic gradient
- Date: 2026-10-09
- Project: local/sandbox-raw-vs-jpeg-tonal-headroom
- Tags: [photography][precision][simulation][compression]
- What: A deterministic synthetic-gradient model of the idea that raw files keep tonal data a JPEG permanently discards. Linear-light ramps are quantised as a 14-bit or 12-bit linear raw, as an 8-bit gamma-encoded JPEG, and (as a control) as an 8-bit linear raw. Each is then pushed or pulled in exposure and rendered to 8-bit output, measuring distinct output codes, coverage of the codes an ideal edit would use, the largest adjacent gap (banding), and the share of pixels piled at 255.
- Why built: The kernel states raw keeps thousands of tonal steps and JPEG only 256, so shadow and highlight recovery differs. A quoted claim is only a candidate, so this tests what survives an edit and where the claim stops holding, with controls that should show no difference.
- Design: Stdlib script, no randomness, log-uniform ramps of 20000 samples. Scenarios: S1 +4 EV push of a ramp 9 to 5 stops below clip; S2 zero-edit control; S3 deep-shadow ramp 12 to 8 stops down pushed +6 EV, built to break the claim; S4 highlight pull with in-camera headroom 0.7, a no-headroom control, and a control for data beyond the sensor clip; S5 8-bit linear raw versus 8-bit gamma JPEG; S6 a warm-light gray ramp whose blue channel is a quarter of green, corrected by a x4 blue gain in post.
- Got right: The direction held everywhere. After the +4 EV push 14-bit raw covered 100% of the output codes with max gap 1, 12-bit covered 77% with gap 4, and the JPEG covered 31% with gap 4 (44 stored levels stretched over 140 codes). At zero edit all paths agreed within 1 code, so the difference only appears when you edit. The histogram reading from the sibling kernel matched: JPEG highlights stored with headroom piled 100% into the 255 bin. The 8-bit linear control covered 6% with gap 71, so bit depth alone is not the story; where the tone curve spends its codes matters.
- Where/why it failed: Two of my numeric predictions were wrong (10 of 12 checks held). (1) I expected raw to keep at least 100 distinct levels after a one-stop highlight pull; it kept 27, because that is all the 8-bit output has room for in the pulled range (coverage 1.00, so the data was fully kept, the threshold was miscalibrated). (2) I expected the JPEG blue channel gap after the x4 gain to be 3 or more; it was 2, though coverage was 0.57 versus 1.00 for raw. The break scenario also showed the claim is narrower than its wording: in deep shadows a 12-bit linear raw stored 16 levels against 13 for an 8-bit gamma JPEG (1.23x), because gamma encoding spends its 8-bit codes on the shadows; only the 14-bit raw (61 levels, 4.7x) was clearly ahead. Raw also does not rescue data beyond the sensor clip: a scene range above 1.0 left at most 1 level in raw too. Limits of a synthetic model: it has no sensor noise (real low bits are partly noise and can dither banding), no lossy block compression, no demosaicing, no camera-specific curve or highlight headroom (the 0.7 headroom is an assumed parameter), and no real raw or JPEG files, so it says what quantisation arithmetic alone predicts and nothing about any specific camera.
- Fix: State the claim as arithmetic about where codes sit: raw advantage = bits recorded in the region you will edit, not the format name. Qualify the kernel's "several times more levels" for shadows when the raw is 12-bit, and keep the highlight claim tied to the camera actually leaving headroom in the raw above the JPEG white point.
- Why this fix: The sandbox separated three causes that the format label bundles together (bit depth, tone-curve placement, headroom), and each control moved only the cause it targeted, so the narrowed statement is what the numbers support.
- Rule of thumb: Edit headroom comes from bits recorded where you will push, not from the word raw. In this model a 14-bit raw gave smooth +4 EV shadow lifts while an 8-bit gamma JPEG banded, but a 12-bit raw was only 1.2x the JPEG in the deepest shadows and an 8-bit linear raw was far worse than the JPEG; data beyond the sensor clip is lost in both. Test any real camera on its own files before relying on this.
