## E1 — rendering shadowbias slope scaled shifts not solves the tradeoff
- Date: 2026-07-13
- Project: local/sandbox-shadowbias
- Tags: [rendering][gamedev][algorithm]
- What: Gap-fill via external research ([rendering] had only 1 entry in this project). Researched the shadow-mapping depth-bias tradeoff (LearnOpenGL, DigitalRune, Microsoft DirectX shadow docs — see Link): a constant depth bias trades shadow acne (false self-shadowing) for peter-panning (visibly detached shadows), is scene/angle-dependent, and slope-scaled bias is commonly recommended as more robust. Built a numeric simulation (quantized shadow-map depth at 16-bit precision, per-texel depth variance growing with surface-to-light angle per real shadow-mapping geometry) to verify this directly rather than take the summary at face value: a constant bias (0.0015) produced acne at 2/90 swept angles (only at extreme grazing, >85°) and a bounded max peter-pan gap of 0.0015. A naive slope-scaled bias (`0.0006 * (1 + tan(theta))`, capped at 0.02) eliminated acne entirely (0/90) but its max peter-pan gap grew to 0.0165 — roughly 11x LARGER than the constant bias ever produced, concentrated at the same extreme grazing angles.
- Why built: To check whether "slope-scaled bias is more robust" (the research summary's headline claim) means strictly better, or whether it's a different tradeoff point on the same acne-vs-peter-panning curve — since the sourced documentation itself notes slope-scaled bias is typically paired with NORMAL-OFFSET bias (a geometrically different technique, moving the sample position rather than scaling the depth-compare margin) for the more robust result, not used alone.
- Design: `receiverErrorAt(theta)` models the real, well-established geometric fact that a shadow-map texel's depth variance grows with `tan(theta)` as the surface approaches grazing incidence to the light. `acne = bias < receiverError` (bias insufficient to cover the real per-texel depth variance, so genuinely-lit fragments misclassify as self-shadowed); `peterPanGap = max(0, bias - receiverError)` (bias exceeding the real variance, visibly detaching the shadow). Swept surface angle 0-89 degrees for both a constant and a slope-scaled bias function.
- Got right: The core mechanism from the research is directly confirmed: constant bias's failure mode (acne) IS concentrated exactly at grazing angles, matching "the right bias is scene-, angle-, and resolution-dependent" — this isn't a vague generalization, it's a specific, reproducible, angle-localized failure.
- Where/why it failed: Slope-scaling the bias by `tan(theta)` alone, without also bounding it against the OTHER failure mode, just moves where on the acne-vs-peter-panning curve the surface sits — because `tan(theta)` grows unboundedly as theta approaches 90°, an unclamped-enough slope-scale factor overcorrects exactly where it's trying hardest to help, producing a peter-panning gap larger than the acne problem it was solving. The research's own recommendation (pair slope-scaled with normal-offset bias) makes sense precisely because normal-offset addresses the SAME grazing-angle problem via a different mechanism (moving the sample position in world space, not just widening a depth-compare margin) that doesn't have this same runaway-at-grazing-angle failure mode.
- Fix: Not implemented as shipping code (a research-verification/decision-layer finding, not a specific engine bug). The verified, portable takeaway: don't adopt "slope-scaled bias" as a drop-in replacement for constant bias expecting a strict improvement — either clamp the slope factor much more conservatively than a naive `1+tan(theta)` formula (verify the clamp empirically across your actual scene's angle range, the same way this sandbox did), or use normal-offset bias (or both together, as sourced documentation recommends) rather than slope-scaling alone.
- Why this fix: A technique summarized as "more robust" in documentation can be robust ALONG ONE AXIS (acne) while being MORE fragile along the other (peter-panning) if adopted in isolation — the summary's brevity elides that the two commonly-paired techniques (slope-scaled, normal-offset) solve different geometric aspects of the same grazing-angle problem, and dropping one while keeping the popular headline claim ("slope-scaled is the fix") reproduces a worse version of the original tradeoff, just shifted.
- Rule of thumb: When external research recommends a technique as "more robust" against a known tradeoff, verify numerically (a small simulation is often enough, as here) whether it eliminates the tradeoff or just relocates it — and check whether the source's actual recommendation is that ONE technique alone, or a COMBINATION (as it was here: slope-scaled + normal-offset) that a compressed summary can make sound like a single fix. Adopting half a two-part recommendation is a common way to import a worse bug than the one being fixed.
- Provenance (verified/assumed): VERIFIED first-hand for the sandbox simulation (swept 90 angles, directly measured acne occurrence and peter-pan gap magnitude for both bias strategies) — the underlying geometric model (depth variance growing with `tan(theta)`) and the "pair with normal-offset" recommendation are ASSUMED from the cited external sources (LearnOpenGL, DigitalRune, Microsoft DirectX docs), not independently re-derived from first principles or tested against a real GPU shadow-mapping implementation.
