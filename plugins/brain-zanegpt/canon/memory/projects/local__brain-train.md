## E1 — round robin dealing vs hash modulo balance vs stability
- Date: 2026-10-09
- Project: local/brain-train
- Tags: [algorithm][determinism][content][testing]
- What: Sandbox measuring the kernel that says to deal a small fixed cast round-robin down a hash-sorted list instead of taking hash modulo N. It compares both schemes on balance (42 ids into 6 buckets), on stability when one id is added or removed or the bucket count changes 6 to 7, and on determinism under input reordering and across processes.
- Why built: The kernel was filed with one anecdote (thirteen of one body type, three of another) and no measurement of its price. It claims "keep determinism, lose the clumping" but does not say what is lost on the other axis, so this run quantifies the trade instead of trusting the story.
- Design: Pure standard library, no RNG. Ids are strings from 200 fixed namespaces; the hash is a wide sha256-derived integer. Modulo deals bucket = hash mod K. Round-robin sorts by (hash, id) and deals position mod K. Metrics: max-min count spread, moved fraction of surviving ids after one add, one remove, and K 6 to 7. Negative controls: round-robin over raw input order, a 6-bit hash key with no id tie-break, the interpreter built-in string hash under two fixed hash seeds in child processes, and a constant bucket function fed to the same balance metric to prove the checker can fail.
- Got right: Round-robin spread was 0 in 200 of 200 namespaces at N=42, K=6 while modulo had median spread 7 and worst 15 (perfect would be 7 per bucket). Modulo relative spread (max-min over mean) fell 1.00 at N=42, 0.30 at N=420, 0.10 at N=4200, so "large enough" really is a hundred times forty. Both content-addressed schemes gave identical assignments under reversed and shuffled input (the sorted deal is as order independent as modulo once ties are broken by id).
- Where/why it failed: The kernel's fix is not free and the kernel does not say so. Adding one id to a population of 42 moved 52.8 percent of the existing ids under round-robin (50.1 percent on removing one) against exactly 0 percent under modulo, because everything sorted after the insertion point shifts one bucket. Changing K from 6 to 7 moved about 86 percent under both, so round-robin has no stability penalty there. Negative controls behaved as built: raw input-order round-robin changed 42 of 42 ids under reversal, the narrow-key sort without a tie-break changed 215 id-bucket pairs across 20 shuffles, and the built-in string hash differed on 34 of 42 ids between two hash seeds. Scope limit: one hash family and one id shape were tried, and the population sizes tested were 42, 420 and 4200.
- Fix: Use hash-sorted round-robin when the cast is small, frozen and balance is visible (parties, fixtures, loot tables, enemy variants). Keep hash modulo when ids arrive one at a time and earlier assignments must never change (persisted per-user variants, sharding), or when the population is in the thousands. Whichever is used, key on a process-independent wide hash and break ties on the id.
- Why this fix: Balance and stability pull opposite ways: an exactly even deal needs to know the whole population, so any population change reshuffles roughly half of it, while modulo of an id-only hash ignores the population and so cannot balance it. The measured 0 versus 53 percent and 7 versus 0 spread numbers show the choice is which of those two costs the product can afford.
- Rule of thumb: Hash-sorted round-robin buys exact balance (spread 0 versus modulo median 7 at 42 ids over 6 buckets) and pays with reassignment (about 50 percent of ids move when one id is added or removed, against 0 for modulo), so freeze the cast before dealing, or keep modulo when assignments must survive growth; in both cases use a stable wide hash plus an id tie-break, since the built-in string hash changed 34 of 42 buckets between processes.
- Provenance (verified/assumed): Verified first-hand by running the attached sandbox, which printed RESULT PASS on 9 of 9 checks and gave byte-identical output on repeat runs; every number above comes from that run. Only the specific hash family, id shape and sizes listed in Where/why it failed were exercised; larger or structured id sets are untested.
