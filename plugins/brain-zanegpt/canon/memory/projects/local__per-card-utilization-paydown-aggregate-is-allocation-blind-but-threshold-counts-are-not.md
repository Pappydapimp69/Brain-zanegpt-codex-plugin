## E1 — per card utilization paydown aggregate is allocation blind but threshold counts are not
- Date: 2026-10-18
- Project: local/per-card-utilization-paydown-aggregate-is-allocation-blind-but-threshold-counts-are-not
- Tags: [personal-finance][testing][model]
- What: With three cards (limits 2000, 5000, 10000; statement balances 1800, 2000, 1200) and 1500 paid before close, aggregate utilization is 20.59 percent for all 475 allocations, always paying the highest-utilization card reaches the lowest possible worst-card utilization (33.0 percent), yet it leaves 3 cards at or above 10 percent and 2 at or above 30 percent where a different split leaves 2 and 1.
- Why built: Train pick applying the credit-utilization kernel (single digits beat 30 percent, and per-card ratios are scored as well as the total), which had only been built against a single card, to a multi-card paydown where the allocation of one budget is the decision.
- Design: Reported utilization is the statement balance over the limit for each card; all allocations of the budget in steps of 50 that do not overpay a card were enumerated; a water-filling policy paid the highest-utilization card 50 at a time; brute force found the minimum worst-card utilization and the minimum count of cards over 10 and over 30 percent.
- Got right: Aggregate utilization was one value across all allocations, water-filling matched the brute-force minimum of the worst-card utilization to 4 decimals, and the threshold counts differed from brute force.
- Where/why it failed: My first idea that equalizing utilization is the best use of the budget holds only for the worst-card objective; when the objective is a count of cards under a cutoff, paying one small card to zero beats balancing, so water-filling gave 3 and 2 against the best 2 and 1. The model counts thresholds as a stand-in for a score and no scoring formula was modeled.
- Fix: Choose the allocation after naming the objective: use water-filling when the worst card matters, and enumerate or pay the cheapest card-to-threshold crossings first when the goal is to get cards under a cutoff; never judge an allocation by the aggregate ratio alone.
- Why this fix: Aggregate utilization depends only on the total paid, so it cannot rank allocations, while the per-card ratios and the threshold counts do depend on the split and the threshold is a step function that favors concentrating payment.
- Rule of thumb: Aggregate utilization cannot compare ways of splitting one paydown across cards; pay the highest-utilization card first to minimize the worst card, but when the target is a count of cards under a cutoff, concentrate the payment on the cards cheapest to push under it.
- Provenance (verified/assumed): Verified first-hand by running the sandbox in this session on one three-card portfolio and one budget; the statement-balance reporting rule is an assumption taken from the earlier entry, and per-card scoring and the cutoffs 10 and 30 percent are stand-ins from the kernel, not checked against a score model.

- ZaneGPT verdict: weak — The aggregate-invariance result and the water-filling optimum for the worst card are solid, but the "concentrate on the cheapest cards" half rests on a single three-card example with hand-picked cutoffs, and it gives no test for when to prefer it over balancing.
- Hidden assumption: Reported utilization is the statement balance over the limit, so that one fixed 1500 prepayment cuts every card's reported balance dollar for dollar. The rule also assumes the only objectives are worst-card utilization and a count at or above a cutoff, and that the 10 and 30 percent cutoffs stand for whatever scoring actually applies. Nobody has checked that any real scoring model uses them.
- Would prove it wrong: A scorer that doesn't depend on those cutoffs would refute it. Examples are per-card bands or a smooth penalty, as in a model that charges extra for any card above 90 percent, or a model that rewards a card reported at zero. If such a model ranks the water-filling split above the "concentrate" split, or the other way round, the second half of the rule has no basis. The same goes for a reading that still has aggregate utilization mattering, such as one that uses it alongside the per-card figures. The "cannot compare" half also needs a check on one more point. If the allocation grid in steps of 50 leaves out a split that does better on the count, the brute-force minimum of 2 and 1 is not actually the minimum.
- Break scenario: Take cards with limits 1000, 1000 and 10000, and statement balances 950, 950 and 500. Budget is 900, all of it on the two small cards. Water-filling spreads it across them, leaving both near 50 percent. Concentrating it takes one card to about 5 percent and leaves the other at 95 percent. The count at or above 30 percent is 1 in both cases, but the worst-card utilization moves from about 50 percent to 95 percent. This shows the "concentrate for counts" advice makes the worst card much worse. It also shows that "cheapest to push under" depends on the cutoff, and that the rule gives no way to pick when a cutoff target and a worst-card target disagree.
