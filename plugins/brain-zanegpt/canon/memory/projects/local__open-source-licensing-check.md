## E1 — no license file default all rights reserved license text sandbox
- Date: 2026-10-09
- Project: local/open-source-licensing-check
- Tags: [legal][licensing][copyright][sandbox][verification]
- What: The legal kernel "no license file means all rights reserved, not free to use" was checked against primary texts and a mechanical model. The Berne Convention (WIPO Lex text) and GitHub's licensing page were fetched as raw HTML, and the GPL-2 and GPL-3 texts in the system license directory were searched. Then a keyword grant detector was run over 14 real license files (corpus part) plus 5 no-license controls (embedded, no files needed). This is not legal advice.
- Why built: The kernel rested on one summarised web page. A first-hand check of the primary rule (copyright exists with no formality, reproduction is reserved to the author) and of how real licenses behave (they are grants, with a prohibition stated underneath) was missing, and so was a test of whether the claim can be checked by machine at all.
- Design: Stdlib Python, no randomness. Part A asserts six verbatim quotes against short excerpts embedded in the .py as string constants, each with a source note: Berne Art 5(2) no formality, Art 9(1) exclusive reproduction right, Art 9(2) special-case exceptions, GitHub's default rule, GPL-2 section 4 and GPL-3 section 8 default prohibitions (in the GPL-3 text on disk the default prohibition is in section 8, Termination, not section 9). Part B is optional and environment-dependent: it needs the system license directory (default /usr/share/common-licenses, override with env var LIC_DIR) and prints SKIPPED if that is missing. Part B splits each of the 14 licenses into sentences and calls a sentence a grant if it has a grant marker, no negation, and a copy, modify or distribute verb. Part C runs 5 controls: an empty file, a rights-reserved notice only, a public README, a view-only text with negation, and informal "free to use" wording. Part D flags copyleft wording. Predictions were written in the file header before the first run.
- Got right: All 6 quotes were found verbatim. 5 of 5 no-license controls granted nothing, including the negation case and the informal-intent case, and a no-license snippet reuse got "no grant, exceptions are a separate case-by-case question" rather than "allowed". GPL-2 section 4 and GPL-3 section 8 state the default as a prohibition, which independently matches the kernel: the license is what lifts it.
- Where/why it failed: My detector predictions were wrong. Predicted 12 of 14 licenses detected as granting copy, modify and distribute; observed 11 of 14 (BSD, CC0-1.0 and MPL-2.0 partial). Cause: the BSD file never says "copy", CC0 grants through a fallback clause worded differently, and my sentence splitter cut MPL-2.0's grant list at a colon so the verbs lost their marker. Predicted 10 copyleft files; the copyleft pattern flagged 6 (GFDL-1.2, GFDL-1.3, GPL-2, GPL-3, MPL-1.1, MPL-2.0) and missed GPL-1, LGPL-2, LGPL-2.1 and LGPL-3, whose wording differs from my pattern. Also out of reach of any keyword model: US statutory text (not fetched), implied licenses, estoppel, and fair use, which the Copyright Office describes as case by case.
- Fix: Treat the primary quotes as the evidence and the detector as a lint only: use it to flag "this text contains no grant", which was reliable on the 5 controls, and never to certify that a text grants a given right or is copyleft. For real license questions read the text and ask a lawyer.
- Why this fix: The claim under test is a negative (nothing is granted), and the controls showed the detector is sound for negatives. Positive classification failed on wording and segmentation, so retuning the regexes until 14 of 14 pass would fit the sample without making the claim better established.
- Rule of thumb: With no license file the default is that the author keeps the exclusive rights (Berne Art 5(2) and 9(1), GitHub, GPL-2 section 4 all say so in their own words), so public does not mean permitted. A keyword model can confirm "no grant found" (5 of 5 controls) but mis-sorted 3 of 14 real licenses on grants and 4 of 10 expected copyleft files, so it cannot say what a license allows. Not legal advice.
- Provenance (verified/assumed): Verified first-hand: Berne Art 5(2), 9(1) and 9(2) wording from a fetched WIPO Lex page; GitHub's default-rule sentence from a fetched docs.github.com page (raw HTML, so the earlier summariser caveat on the kernel is resolved for that sentence); GPL-2 section 4 and GPL-3 section 8 from /usr/share/common-licenses (now also embedded in the .py as excerpts and re-checked against the files at filing time); and every sandbox number. Reproducibility: the six quotes and the 5 controls reproduce anywhere from the .py alone; the 14-license grant and copyleft numbers (11/14, 6 flagged) depend on the system license directory contents and can differ on a machine with other or no license texts. Assumed or taken from the kernel text only: fair-use doctrine, the US Copyright Office statements, and how courts treat implied licenses.
