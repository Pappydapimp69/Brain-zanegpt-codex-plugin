## E2 — encounter introduction causal runway
- Date: 2026-08-30
- Project: local/b
- Tags: [narrative-design][causality][character][setup-payoff]
- What: Replaced biography-shaped witness introductions with a forward-link ledger that admits a detail only when it causally prepares the encounter through placement, perception, action, stake, or the reason for testimony.
- Why built: A fictional Sasquatch witness introduction improved markedly in oral cadence but wandered into a present-day billing job, an affinity for numbers, marriage history, and a sister's illness. Some material was emotionally plausible, yet it did not consistently lead into or alter the encounter the book promised.
- Design: Treat the introduction as a causal runway. Before drafting, map each proposed detail to one or more downstream roles: Placement explains why the witness is at the location and time; Perception changes what they notice or misread; Action enables or motivates an encounter choice; Stake identifies what the encounter threatens; Testimony explains concealment, preservation, doubt, or telling now. Require a concrete later payoff that changes behavior, interpretation, evidence, danger, or disclosure.
- Got right: The revised voice had more natural hesitation, self-correction, listener awareness, and reconstructive memory. The sister was in fact integral to the planned encounter and later reason for telling, showing that family history can belong when it carries real structural weight.
- Where/why it failed: Character specificity was mistaken for narrative relevance. The billing-and-numbers detail sounded like purposeful characterization but had no event-level link to the encounter. Research distinguishes causal connection from semantic similarity: thematically apt material does not by itself help readers build the story's causal model. The introduction advertised connections the later story was not designed to pay.
- Fix: Apply the forward-link ledger and deletion test. For each biographical fact, name the exact encounter beat or testimony decision it changes. If removing the fact leaves the reader's understanding and the witness's behavior unchanged, cut it. Preserve an occupation only when it places the witness, supplies relevant expertise or limitation, drives action, affects evidence, or changes credibility. Preserve family only when they cause placement, participate, are at stake, change interpretation, or motivate telling.
- Why this fix: Narrative-comprehension research finds causal structure contributes to comprehension beyond semantic similarity, and events with more causal connections are judged more important and remembered better. The method also operationalizes Brain's existing idea that a spotlighted detail is a promise: the ledger defines what counts as payment rather than trusting authorial intuition.
- Rule of thumb: An encounter introduction is a causal runway, not a biography — every detail must change why the witness is there, what they perceive, what they do, what is at stake, or why they tell it.
- Provenance (verified/assumed): Verified first-hand through the user's 3/10 evaluation and specific identification of the disconnected numbers detail. Verified research basis through Lee et al. on causal narrative integration; Chen et al. on causal structure and memory; a causal-inference review; and the Event Indexing Model. The five-role ledger has been applied to all five private profiles but remains to be validated in the next reader-facing sample.

## E1 — oral testimony disfluency
- Date: 2026-08-30
- Project: local/b
- Tags: [narrative-design][voice][oral-history][disfluency]
- What: Rebuilt fictional first-person encounter stories as interview-born oral testimony whose hesitations, repetitions, cutoffs, and self-repairs arise from live memory retrieval and interaction with a listener.
- Why built: A polished first-person Sasquatch encounter was intended to feel like a witness speaking from memory, but it instead sounded written, rehearsed, and generically AI-generated despite having a detailed private character profile.
- Design: Give every narrator an interview situation and a delivery fingerprint alongside the character profile: who is listening, why the narrator agreed to speak, what is at risk, their topic-specific fluency, repair phrases, resistance triggers, interaction with questions, chronology breaks, and a late detail that revises an earlier claim. Draft the live interview first, then omit only interviewer turns whose pressure remains audible in the answer.
- Got right: The underlying profile differentiated biography, expertise, belief, perception, emotional baselines, encounter design, and aftermath. Those dimensions supplied credible content and potential voice material.
- Where/why it failed: The prose arrived in balanced sentences, complete metaphors, clean chronology, and prepared rhetorical turns. Disfluency was absent, but merely adding fillers would also fail: research on spontaneous speech shows that random insertion and repetition do not resemble natural disfluency. The missing layer was the interactional event and the cognitive work of reconstructing memory.
- Fix: Make each speech disturbance causal. Retrieval searches for a fact; repair corrects meaning or social exposure; monitoring responds to the listener's likely interpretation; avoidance approaches and retreats from dangerous material; interruption changes the thought path; reorientation supplies belated context; insistence repeats what must be understood. Let general context arrive early and event-specific sensory or emotional details accumulate, sometimes correcting the initial account. Audit the voice with punctuation normalized so identity cannot depend on dashes and fillers.
- Why this fix: Oral-history guidance treats narration as a dynamic collaboration shaped by interviewer and narrator. Autobiographical-memory research supports reconstructive, progressively detailed recall. Conversation research treats fillers and self-repair as functional signals, while computational disfluency research directly finds random insertion unnatural. The fix models causes rather than cosmetic symptoms.
- Rule of thumb: Never make polished prose sound oral by damaging it afterward; construct the act of remembering in front of a listener, then let every interruption reveal retrieval, repair, relationship, or resistance.
- Provenance (verified/assumed): Verified first-hand that the polished-monologue approach failed the user's believability test. Verified the revised design against Oral History Association best practices; Clark (1994); Clark and Fox Tree (2002); Yang, Yang, and Ma (2020); and autobiographical-memory studies on unfolding detail and spoken versus typed recall. The effectiveness of the revised scaffold on a complete story remains to be tested.
