## E5 — front loaded checksum needs chunk seq and total
- Date: 2026-10-09
- Project: local/sandbox-checksums
- Tags: [checksums][streaming][security][determinism][testing]
- What: Stress-test of the "front-load the checksum so a stream can be verified with a small buffer" lesson against the chunk-decoding lesson, the explicit-field lesson and the forgery lesson. Six wire schemes (trailing whole-stream CRC, leading whole-stream CRC, per-chunk CRC, per-chunk CRC plus sequence number, plus a declared chunk total, and a keyed per-chunk MAC over seq+total+data) were run over a 10,000-byte multibyte UTF-8 payload in 500-byte chunks against six faults.
- Why built: The streaming lesson says a leading checksum plus a declared length bounds peak buffer to the chunk size. That is true for memory, but it is silent about what the receiver may do with bytes before the verdict, and about what a per-chunk check does and does not bind.
- Design: Deterministic, stdlib only (zlib crc32, hmac). Faults: clean control, one flipped byte in chunk 0, a dropped middle chunk, two swapped chunks, three chunks truncated off the tail, and an attacker who rewrites chunk 7 and recomputes every UNKEYED checksum. A separate sweep decodes the verified chunks as UTF-8 at wire chunk sizes 1..40, once per chunk independently and once with an incremental decoder.
- Got right: Peak buffer is as the streaming lesson says: 10000 for trailing-whole, 500 for every leading or per-chunk scheme. Per-chunk checks reject a corrupted first chunk at chunk 0 and release 0 bytes. The keyed MAC with seq and total is the only scheme that rejected all five faults.
- Where/why it failed: Three things the buffer number hides. (1) Leading whole-stream checksum bounds memory but not exposure: with a flipped byte in chunk 0 it handed all 10000 bytes downstream and only rejected at the end, while trailing-whole released 0. (2) Per-chunk CRC alone is blind to ordering and completeness: a dropped chunk, two swapped chunks and a truncated tail were all ACCEPTED (3 of 3), and adding a sequence number still ACCEPTED the truncated tail because the end of the stream is not self-announcing; only a declared total caught it. (3) Every unkeyed scheme, including the best CRC one, ACCEPTED the attacker rewrite (5 of 5). Separately, once chunks pass verification, decoding each independently as UTF-8 corrupted clean text at 37 of 40 chunk sizes (safe only at 11, 22, 33); an incremental decoder corrupted 0 of 40.
- Fix: Choose the framing by what must be bounded. If downstream must never see unverified bytes, use per-chunk tags and carry a sequence number and a declared total count in the header (the explicit-field rule applied to completeness); if the threat includes an attacker, key the tag (MAC over seq, total and data). Carry decoder state across verified chunks instead of decoding each chunk alone.
- Why this fix: The sandbox shows each added field closes exactly one named hole (seq closes drop and swap, total closes tail truncation, key closes forgery), and none of them costs buffer: all stayed at 500. The whole-stream leading checksum is still fine when the consumer can stage the data and discard it on a failed verdict.
- Rule of thumb: Peak buffer is only half of what checksum placement decides: a leading whole-stream checksum releases the whole payload before any verdict (10000 of 10000 bytes here), and per-chunk CRCs fix that but silently accept dropped, reordered and truncated chunks unless the header also carries a sequence number and a declared total, and any unkeyed check still accepts a deliberate rewrite. Tested on six schemes and six faults, one payload shape; verify chunks on raw bytes and decode incrementally across them.
- Provenance (verified/assumed): Verified first-hand: all numbers above come from the saved sandbox run (RESULT PASS, 8 of 8 checks matching the written prediction). The streaming and forgery claims were measured on a simulated in-memory wire with one payload and one chunk size of 500; real transports, other payload shapes and larger chunk counts were not exercised.

## E4 — order insensitive checksums miss burst and transposition errors
- Date: 2026-09-29
- Project: local/sandbox-checksums
- Tags: [checksums][determinism][testing]
- What: An 8-bit additive-sum and an 8-bit XOR checksum both miss real error classes a CRC32 catches outright. (1) Against 2000 simulated 4-byte burst corruptions (adjacent-byte errors, the realistic shape of a storage/transmission glitch, not independent random bit flips), sum8 missed 10/2000 (0.50%) and xor8 missed 12/2000 (0.60%); crc32 missed 0/2000. (2) Both sum8 and xor8 are provably blind to byte TRANSPOSITION — swapping two bytes in a 30-byte string leaves both checksums unchanged, because addition and XOR are commutative and don't encode byte POSITION at all; crc32 (position-sensitive by construction) detects the same swap every time.
- Why built: `checksums` was fully empty in canon (0 memory, 0 ideas). Sum/XOR checksums are the cheapest, most obviously-reached-for implementation (no library needed), so it's worth establishing concretely how much real error-detection they give up relative to a CRC, rather than leaving "checksums exist" undifferentiated.
- Design: Deterministic Python (`sandbox_checksums.py` loops 1-2), seeded RNG (seed 42) for loop 1 only, used purely to generate a large, reproducible sample of burst-corruption positions/magnitudes — the checksum comparison itself is exact arithmetic, not sampled. Loop 2 is a single hand-constructed transposition, no randomness.
- Got right: All three checksums correctly detect ordinary independent random single-byte corruption at close to their theoretical rate; the gap only opens on the two structured error classes (burst/adjacent, and transposition) that real corruption (a stuck bit region, a copy/paste reorder bug, a network reordering) actually produces.
- Where/why it failed: Addition and XOR are both commutative and position-blind — they reduce a byte sequence to "some combination of the values present," discarding WHERE each byte sat, which is exactly the information a transposition changes and burst corruption partially preserves (nearby bytes changing together can partially cancel in a sum).
- Fix: Use a position-sensitive check (CRC, or any polynomial/rolling checksum) wherever the corruption model includes anything more structured than independent random bit noise — which is most real corruption. Reserve sum/XOR checksums for contexts where the only threat is scattered independent bit noise and detection strength doesn't matter much (a cheap "did this obviously get truncated" sanity check, not a data-integrity guarantee).
- Why this fix: The weakness isn't a tuning problem (a wider sum wouldn't fix transposition-blindness — any commutative combining function is blind to order by definition); the fix has to change the algorithm class, not the checksum width.
- Rule of thumb: Before trusting a checksum's error-detection claim, ask whether the algorithm is POSITION-SENSITIVE — a commutative one (plain sum, XOR) is provably blind to any corruption that permutes or rearranges bytes without changing which values are present, independent of how many bits wide it is.
- Provenance (verified/assumed): Verified first-hand — sandbox built and run; the printed miss counts (10/2000, 12/2000, 0/2000) and the transposition test's three boolean results are observed output.

## E3 — checksum placement trades against streaming buffer cost
- Date: 2026-09-29
- Project: local/sandbox-checksums
- Tags: [checksums][streaming][testing]
- What: Where a checksum sits in a frame directly sets the receiver's peak buffer requirement for streaming verification. A trailing (suffix) checksum forces buffering the ENTIRE payload before it can be checked at all — in a 10,000-byte payload split into 500-byte chunks, peak buffer was the full 10,000 bytes. A leading (prefix) checksum, paired with a declared length up front, lets a receiver feed an incremental hash and discard each chunk as it's consumed — peak buffer drops to one chunk (500 bytes), a 20x reduction in this test, and the reduction scales with payload size, not chunk size.
- Why built: Checksum placement (prefix vs suffix) is usually chosen for format-authoring convenience, not for its downstream cost on the receiving side; worth making the tradeoff concrete and measured rather than leaving it as an unexamined default (most simple formats append the checksum, which is also the worse case for streaming).
- Design: Deterministic Python (`sandbox_checksums.py` loop 5), no RNG. A 10,000-byte payload split into 500-byte chunks; a suffix-verification path that must accumulate all chunks before the checksum function can run; a prefix-plus-declared-length path where the receiver can, in principle, feed an incremental hasher and drop each chunk after consuming it (the toy implementation still computes crc32 over the full buffer for correctness, but tracks and reports peak per-chunk retention separately to isolate the actual streaming-relevant quantity).
- Got right: Both placements verify correctly when given the complete data (crc32 match confirmed for both paths) — the lesson is purely about RESOURCE cost during verification, not about either placement being wrong or right for correctness.
- Where/why it failed: A suffix checksum is a structural, not incidental, streaming blocker — the verifier CANNOT know whether the data it has seen so far is complete until it reads past all of it to reach the checksum, so there is no way to start discarding buffered bytes early no matter how the receiver is implemented, only ways to reduce how painful the eventual full buffer is (e.g., an incremental/rolling checksum still needs the checksum VALUE before it can conclude, and that value is the last thing to arrive).
- Fix: For any streaming or memory-constrained receiver, prefer a checksum-then-length-then-data (or otherwise front-loaded) layout paired with an incremental hash function, so verification can proceed chunk-by-chunk and buffered bytes can be released as soon as they're hashed — reserve trailing checksums for formats that are always read as a complete, already-buffered unit (a file loaded fully into memory, not a network stream being processed as it arrives).
- Why this fix: The cost isn't in the checksum algorithm's CPU cost (crc32 is cheap either way) — it's in the MEMORY lifetime the placement forces on every byte of the payload, which is a structural property of where the last piece of information (the checksum value) needed to trust the data actually arrives relative to the data itself.
- Rule of thumb: Before choosing where a checksum goes in a format, ask whether anything will ever need to verify it while streaming (not fully buffered) — if so, a trailing checksum forces buffering the whole message regardless of chunk size, while a leading checksum plus a declared length lets verification proceed incrementally with buffer cost bounded by chunk size, not message size.
- Provenance (verified/assumed): Verified first-hand — sandbox built and run; the printed peak-buffer figures (10000 bytes vs 500 bytes) and both paths' correctness (crc32 match confirmed) are observed output, not estimates.

## E2 — a checksum is not tamper evident
- Date: 2026-09-29
- Project: local/sandbox-checksums
- Tags: [checksums][security][testing]
- What: Forging a payload with a different meaning but an identical 8-bit sum checksum took exactly one free padding byte, solved with a single subtraction — take the target checksum, compute the forged payload's own sum, and set the padding byte to the difference mod 256. The resulting forged payload's sum8 matches the original exactly; the SAME forged payload's crc32 does not match, because crc32 mixes every input byte nonlinearly through a fixed polynomial rather than combining them with an invertible, single-step arithmetic operation.
- Why built: "The checksum matches, so the message is intact" is an easy, wrong inference to make informally, and it's worth a concrete, quantified demonstration of exactly how cheap breaking that inference is for the class of checksum most likely to be reached for by default (an additive sum) — one padding byte and one subtraction, not a search or a brute force.
- Design: Deterministic Python (`sandbox_checksums.py` loop 3), no RNG. A 20-byte original payload's sum8 is computed as the target; a forged payload with a materially different meaning (a changed dollar amount) has one appended byte solved algebraically to match that target sum; both payloads' crc32 is also computed for comparison.
- Got right: The forgery is not probabilistic or approximate — the solved padding byte makes sum8 match EXACTLY, on the first attempt, because an 8-bit additive sum is a linear function of the input bytes and therefore has a closed-form inverse for any single free byte.
- Where/why it failed: The property that makes additive/XOR checksums cheap to compute (they're linear, order-independent combining functions) is the exact same property that makes them trivially invertible — given a target value and one degree of freedom, solving for the byte that produces it is arithmetic, not search. A checksum's job (cheap accidental-corruption detection) and a MAC/signature's job (expensive, computationally-infeasible-to-forge tamper evidence) are different problems with different required algorithm properties, and a checksum's design explicitly optimizes away the property (non-invertibility) that tamper evidence needs.
- Fix: Never use a plain checksum (sum, XOR, CRC, or any non-cryptographic combining function) as evidence a message wasn't deliberately altered by an adversary with write access to the message — that requires a keyed MAC or a signature, where forging a match requires either the secret key or breaking the underlying cryptographic primitive, not solving a linear equation.
- Why this fix: CRC32 (position-sensitive, not linear over the message the same simple way) resisted this SPECIFIC one-byte-padding attack in this test, but that resistance is incidental to CRC's error-detection design goal, not a security property it was built or vetted for — CRC collisions are still easy to construct with slightly more effort (CRC is a linear code over GF(2), so an attacker who controls more than one byte can solve for a match the same way, just with different arithmetic); the fix is choosing the right PROBLEM CLASS of algorithm (cryptographic, keyed) for the tamper-evidence use case, not picking a specific non-cryptographic checksum that happened to resist one attack.
- Rule of thumb: A checksum answers "did this get corrupted by accident," never "was this deliberately changed by someone who can see the checksum" — the moment an adversary can read (and especially write near) the data before you verify it, only a keyed/cryptographic scheme (HMAC, a signature) answers the second question, and reaching for "the checksum matches" as reassurance against tampering is a category error regardless of which checksum algorithm was chosen.
- Provenance (verified/assumed): Verified first-hand — sandbox built and run; the forged payload's sum8-match (True) and crc32-mismatch (False, i.e. correctly caught) are observed output from an actual constructed forgery, not a theoretical claim.

## E1 — a checksum field needs an explicit presence flag
- Date: 2026-09-29
- Project: local/sandbox-checksums
- Tags: [checksums][protocol-design][testing]
- What: A frame format that appends a checksum byte with no explicit flag or length field marking its presence makes a CORRUPTED checksum byte indistinguishable from a CHECKSUM-LESS frame that happens to be one byte longer for an unrelated reason — a receiver that only knows the expected payload length sees "one extra byte" in both cases and has no way to tell "trust this extra byte as a (possibly wrong) checksum" apart from "this sender doesn't use checksums and this extra byte is something else entirely."
- Why built: Appending a checksum with no format marker is the natural first implementation (no schema/versioning needed yet), and the failure only appears once a SECOND thing can also produce an extra trailing byte (a checksum-less mode, a future format extension, a truncated/corrupted transmission) — worth establishing concretely rather than leaving "just append a checksum" as an unexamined default.
- Design: Deterministic Python (`sandbox_checksums.py` loop 4), no RNG. Two frame constructors (with and without a trailing checksum byte) and a receiver-side length check that only knows the expected PAYLOAD length, not whether a checksum should be present; a checksum byte is corrupted (XORed) and checked against the same ambiguous "extra byte present" signal a checksum-less frame would also trigger.
- Got right: The receiver's length-based ambiguity check correctly reports "extra byte present" for the corrupted-checksum frame and correctly reports "no extra byte" for the genuinely checksum-less frame in THIS test — the two cases are only ambiguous relative to each other when both are live possibilities in the same protocol version, which is exactly the scenario the lesson is about.
- Where/why it failed: The frame FORMAT has an implicit invariant ("if there's an extra byte, it's a checksum") that isn't checked anywhere in the wire format itself — a single flipped bit in the checksum byte doesn't change its LENGTH, so a receiver checking "does this look like a checksummed frame" by shape alone can't distinguish a real-but-wrong checksum from a byte that was never meant to be one under a different mode.
- Fix: Reserve an explicit presence indicator (a version/flags byte, a fixed header field) rather than inferring checksum presence from frame length alone — the indicator itself doesn't need to be checksummed against corruption any more robustly than the rest of the header, but its EXISTENCE removes the shape-based ambiguity entirely, since the receiver then always knows which interpretation to apply before looking at the trailing bytes.
- Why this fix: The ambiguity isn't a rare edge case triggered by unlikely inputs — it's guaranteed the moment a protocol has (or will ever have) more than one valid frame shape, because length alone can't carry two independent pieces of information (payload length AND checksum-mode) without a dedicated field for the second one.
- Rule of thumb: Before shipping a checksum appended/prepended with no format marker, ask whether the protocol will ever have (now or in a future version) any OTHER reason for the frame to be a different length — if so, a corrupted checksum and a checksum-less frame become indistinguishable by shape, and an explicit presence flag is required, not optional polish.
- Provenance (verified/assumed): Verified first-hand — sandbox built and run; the printed ambiguity results (corrupted-checksum frame: extra byte detected = True; checksum-less frame: extra byte detected = False) are observed output from the actual constructed frames.
